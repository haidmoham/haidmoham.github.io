Experiment #05 · Part 1 · Open

Static support: an honest pause

A tripod can look upright in a screenshot and still be on the verge of tipping. Part 1 replaces that visual guess with measurements, then stops when a configuration error makes the later results unusable.

01 · Question

When is a tripod pose physically supported?

I wanted measurements that could distinguish support from appearance. The robot and ballast share one combined center of mass, and gravity acts downward through that point. The feet touching the ground define a support triangle. Projecting the center of mass vertically onto the ground shows where gravity’s line of action falls relative to that triangle.

02 · Prediction

The centered ballast should remain inside the support triangle.

For the centered 0.45 kg baseline, I expected the center-of-mass projection to remain inside the triangle, all three feet to keep carrying upward load, and the body to stay nearly level. That prediction applies only to this baseline. I did not record a clean prediction for a corrected attached-ballast sweep.

03 · Experiment

Measure support instead of trusting a pose.

The MuJoCo model had a free-moving torso and three articulated legs. Its leg motors reused the test bench’s existing controller to hold the intended tripod posture, so this was a test of support under that controller rather than passive balance. Each run recorded the combined center of mass, its ground projection, which feet were touching, the upward load at each foot, distance to the nearest support edge, body orientation, angular velocity, and body height.

The viewer overlaid a translucent no-ballast reference for inspection. The numerical report supplied the evidence.

Engineering detour · viewer architecture

The first telemetry layout attached six live plots to the MuJoCo viewer. Reducing how often I published new plot data did not remove the rendering cost because MuJoCo still drew every attached figure on every video frame.

The useful decisions came from reading MuJoCo’s passive-viewer documentation and upstream viewer loop. The passive viewer leaves timing and synchronization to the calling script. Upstream, the loop advances fixed physics steps until simulation time catches up with wall time, and the viewer offers a faster state-only synchronization path when the model itself has not changed. Those patterns separated the timing problem from the plot-rendering problem.

I adapted them into three independent rates. Physics kept its fixed simulation timestep. The scene synchronized to wall-clock time at 60 frames per second. Telemetry sampled and refreshed at 10 Hz. The viewer displayed one page of three plots at a time while continuing to record every signal, including those on the hidden page.

That debugging produced shared playback, rate-gating, and telemetry-paging helpers for later experiments. It made the viewer easier to inspect without changing the numerical report or turning the visualization into evidence.

04 · Observation

The centered baseline behaved as predicted; the later sweep did not survive inspection.

The centered 0.45 kg baseline kept all three feet in contact. Its center-of-mass projection stayed inside the support triangle, and its roll and pitch remained near zero during the fixed-duration run.

I later varied a forward ballast from 1 to 100 kg. The results seemed to show a sharp tipping threshold, but the viewer exposed a configuration error. I had entered the ballast height as a world coordinate even though MuJoCo interpreted it relative to the chassis. The ballast was suspended far above the robot instead of resting on top of it.

Worth noting

The mass sweep is useful only as a configuration failure. Once the viewer contradicted the intended setup, its apparent tipping threshold stopped being evidence about attached-ballast stability.

05 · Model update

The active feet define the support region.

The active ground contacts form a support polygon. With three feet down, that polygon is the triangle joining them. In quiet static equilibrium on level ground, the upward forces at the feet must add up to the robot’s weight, and their moments must cancel the moment produced by gravity. That balance is possible while the center-of-mass projection lies inside the triangle or on its boundary.

If the projection passes beyond an edge, balancing the robot would require the opposite foot to pull downward on the ground. A normal contact force cannot pull, so that foot unloads. The two contacts along the crossed edge can then become a pivot, and gravity produces a tipping moment about that edge.

This is the mechanics model for the next experiment. Part 1 did not verify that sequence with a correctly attached ballast.

06 · Continuation boundary

Practice first; resume the experiment afterward.

Before running another treatment, I need practice with static equilibrium, one-way contact forces, moments about a pivot edge, and rigid-body angular velocity. I will use problems from the second edition of Daniel Kleppner and Robert Kolenkow’s An Introduction to Mechanics. That practice prepares Part 2; it is not Part 2.

Part 2 starts afterward, with a new prediction and a ballast position specified relative to the chassis. Until then, the experiment remains open. Neither this tripod nor C-1N has demonstrated stability with an attached ballast.
